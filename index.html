Background

What is a 10-K — and where does ADAM look?

Form 10-KAnnual SEC filing for U.S.-listed public companies. It combines business description, risks, management discussion, and audited financial statements.
Item 1A — Risk FactorsPotential threats, exposures, uncertainties, and adverse future conditions. Negative language is often the baseline rather than the exception.
Item 7 — MD&AManagement's discussion of performance, liquidity, capital resources, known trends, and expected developments.

Why these two sections?

  • Both are narrative-heavy and decision-relevant.
  • They have different linguistic baselines.
  • A negative phrase can mean something very different in Risk Factors than in MD&A.

Source: SEC Form 10-K structure; focal sections in the ADAM study.

Audience check

Item 1A or Item 7? Vote before I reveal the answer

Passage A

“Changes in tax law or trade rules in the countries where we operate could materially increase our costs or limit our ability to sell.”

Passage B

“Revenue rose this year because volumes increased, partly offset by higher costs; liquidity remains adequate, but input prices could affect future margins.”

A → Item 1A (Risk Factors). It states a potential threat — tax or trade rules could raise costs or limit sales. That is the language of risk, not a report on performance.

B → Item 7 (MD&A). It reports this year's performance — revenue, volumes, costs, liquidity — and what could affect future margins. That is management discussing results.

Motivation

Why narrative disclosures are hard to analyze

VolumeNarrative sections are long. Paragraph-by-paragraph review is costly and attention is scarce.
RepetitionNew information is mixed with prior-year carryover, boilerplate, safe-harbor language, and legal caution.
Interpretive difficultyTone, economic direction, and informativeness are related — but they are not the same construct.

A paragraph can be linguistically negative, economically neutral, and low in new information — all at the same time.

DSS question: Which passages contain new, firm-specific, decision-relevant information — and which passages warrant closer human judgment?

Method 1

Loughran–McDonald: a finance-specific dictionary

What LM does

  1. Tokenize the paragraph
  2. Match words to finance-specific lists
  3. Count category hits
  4. Normalize by total words

Key categories: Positive · Negative · Uncertainty · Litigious · Constraining · Modal

Net tone
(Positive − Negative) ÷ Total words

Strength: transparent and auditable.

Limitation: counts words, not meaning.

Do not confuse it with Henry's measure. (Positive − Negative) ÷ (Positive + Negative) is Henry (2008)'s tone measure — not LM 2011's formula. In the worked example two cards ahead (5 positive, 6 negative words), Henry's measure gives (5−6)/(5+6) = −0.091 — same sign as LM's −0.02, different magnitude.

Source: Loughran & McDonald (2011).

Method 2

FinBERT: contextual sentiment for financial language

What FinBERT adds

FinBERT reads words in relation to surrounding text. It returns:

  • P(Negative)
  • P(Neutral)
  • P(Positive)
  • Predicted class
  • Model confidence

Illustrative sentence

“Revenue fell sharply as costs rose and demand weakened.”

0.62Negative
0.30Neutral
0.08Positive

Predicted class = argmax; confidence = max probability.

Source: Araci (2019); illustrative probabilities shown here.

Audience challenge · Interactive

Read FinBERT's output — your call

Suppose FinBERT returns: Negative 0.62 · Neutral 0.30 · Positive 0.08.

1. What class is predicted?

2. What is the confidence?

Predicted class: Negative. 0.62 is the largest probability.

Confidence: 0.62. The winning probability, not the sum. At 0.62 it routes to human review — ADAM's review threshold is 0.80.

3. What FinBERT still can NOT answer: Is the language new? Repeated? Peer-common? Firm-specific?

Think: tone ≠ informativeness.

FinBERT answers: “What does this paragraph sound like?”

Conceptual pivot

Why disagreement can be useful evidence

“The company is not expected to face a material loss from the pending litigation this year.”

LM says: NEGATIVE
Matched: loss · litigation
vs
FinBERT says: NEUTRAL
Read: “not expected to”

The disagreement is explainable: lexical negativity + semantic negation.

Agreement does not guarantee correctness; disagreement does not necessarily imply ambiguity.

Research design

Four questions, four design requirements

Research questions

  • RQ1 — How can a DSS coordinate lexicon, transformer, and contextual-quality evidence?
  • RQ2 — Does adjudication improve interpretation — and is the gain architectural?
  • RQ3 — What explains disagreement, and does it identify passages needing review?
  • RQ4 — Do confidence-aware outputs support prioritization and selective human review?

Design requirements

  • DR1 — Preserve heterogeneous evidence
  • DR2 — Diagnose disagreement
  • DR3 — Qualify signals with temporal / disclosure context
  • DR4 — Maintain traceability to source text

Design-science logic: architecture, artifact evaluation, and decision-support usefulness.

Artifact

ADAM: a six-layer decision-support architecture

  1. Disclosure & Decision Context — 10-K Item 1A / Item 7 · firm · year · section · sector
  2. Structuring & Contextualization — section extraction · paragraph objects · prior-year / peer links
  3. Specialized Evidence Agents — LM lexicon · FinBERT · Context & Quality
  4. Intelligent Adjudication — assess convergence · diagnose conflict · retain unresolved cases
  5. Disclosure Intelligence Synthesis — paragraph → section → company-year measures
  6. Decision-Support Outputs — profiles · alerts · drill-down · evidence · explanations

Agentic ≠ every component is an LLM. Agentic = specialized roles + structured evidence + explicit coordination.

Evidence generation

Three agents — deliberately non-equivalent roles

LM Lexicon AgentEvidence: Positive / negative · Uncertainty · Litigiousness · Constraining / modal.
Strength: transparent and auditable.
Limit: weak semantic context.
FinBERT AgentEvidence: P(Negative) · P(Neutral) · P(Positive) · predicted class · model confidence.
Strength: semantic context.
Limit: lower direct traceability.
Context & Quality AgentEvidence: persistence / novelty · peer commonality · specificity · boilerplate · hedging / negation / caution.
Strength: disclosure context.

Outputs remain separate until adjudication.

Context

What the Context & Quality Agent adds

  • Year-over-year similarity — Is the paragraph substantially carried over from the prior filing?
  • Peer commonality — Is the language common in the sector or unusually firm-specific?
  • Multi-year persistence — Does similar language recur across t−1 and t−2?
  • Specificity — Does the passage contain concrete firm-specific details?
  • Temporal novelty — Is comparable wording absent from recent disclosure history?
  • Boilerplate / qualifiers — Is the passage repetitive, hedged, conditional, negated, or legally cautious?

Key distinction: sentiment asks “what is the tone?”; context asks “how informative is this language here and now?”

Reasoning

Adjudication separates tone, economics, informativeness, and uncertainty

Sentiment: Positive · Negative · Neutral Economic direction: Favorable · Adverse · Mixed · No clear direction Informativeness: High · Medium · Low Decision status: confidence · ambiguity · review-needed

Six primary discrepancy diagnoses:

  1. No meaningful conflict
  2. Lexical–semantic mismatch
  3. Boilerplate / generic language
  4. Factual event vs. neutral wording
  5. Conditional / negated / mixed language
  6. Unresolved evidence

Adjudication uses only filing-time text and context. Market outcomes remain external evaluation variables.

Evaluation

Separate model performance from architectural contribution

Conventional benchmarks

  • LM dictionary — lexical baseline
  • FinBERT — contextual baseline
  • LM + FinBERT — simple non-agentic ensemble

Nested architecture tests

  • Judge only — same adjudicator + paragraph only
  • Base evidence — paragraph + LM + FinBERT
  • Full ADAM — + Context & Quality evidence

What we evaluate: accuracy + macro-F1 · class-level precision / recall · agreement vs disagreement cases · adjudicator behavior · human-disagreement alignment · confidence-based review · external validation (ongoing).

Hold the adjudicator constant → isolate the value of the evidence architecture.
